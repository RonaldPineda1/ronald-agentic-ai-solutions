How to Boil Water
Boiling water is a simple process that involves heating water until it reaches its boiling point (212°F or 100°C at sea level). Here's how to do it:
On the stovetop:
First, fill a pot or kettle with the amount of water you need. Use cold tap water for the best results. Place the pot on a burner and turn the heat to high. If you're using a pot, you can cover it with a lid to speed up the process, though this is optional.
Watch for signs that the water is heating up. You'll first see small bubbles forming on the bottom of the pot - this is called nucleate boiling. As the temperature increases, these bubbles will grow larger and rise to the surface. When you see a continuous stream of large bubbles breaking at the surface and steam rising vigorously, the water has reached a full rolling boil.
In an electric kettle:
Simply fill the kettle with water up to the indicated maximum line, place it on its base, and press the power button. The kettle will automatically shut off when the water reaches boiling point.
In the microwave (less recommended):
While possible, this method requires caution due to the risk of superheating. Use a microwave-safe container and heat in 1-2 minute intervals, stirring between each interval. Be very careful when removing it as the water can suddenly boil over.
The time it takes to boil water depends on the volume of water, the starting temperature, the heat source's power, and your altitude (water boils at lower temperatures at higher elevations).

I'll walk you through boiling water step by step - it's one of the most fundamental cooking skills you'll use constantly in the kitchen.
What You'll Need:

A pot or saucepan (choose a size appropriate for how much water you need)
Water (cold tap water is fine)
A stove or electric burner
A lid for the pot (optional, but speeds up the process)

Step-by-Step Instructions:
First, fill your pot with the amount of water you need. Don't fill it all the way to the top - leave at least an inch or two of space to prevent overflow when the water bubbles. For most purposes, filling the pot about two-thirds full works well.
Place the pot on a burner that matches its size. If your pot is small, use a small burner; this helps heat efficiently and prevents the flame from licking up the sides of the pot if you're using gas.
Turn the heat to high. This is where beginners sometimes hesitate, but for boiling water, you want maximum heat to get there quickly. If you have a lid, put it on the pot - this traps heat and can cut your waiting time significantly.
Now you wait and watch. You'll notice different stages as the water heats up. First, tiny bubbles will form on the bottom of the pot - this isn't boiling yet. Then you'll see small bubbles rising to the surface and steam starting to form. Eventually, you'll reach a full rolling boil where large bubbles continuously break the surface and the water is actively churning.
How to Know When It's Ready:
Water boils at 212°F (100°C) at sea level. You'll know you've reached a full boil when you see continuous, vigorous bubbling that doesn't stop when you stir the water. The steam will be steady and pronounced.
Once your water is boiling, you can add whatever you're cooking - pasta, vegetables, eggs - or use it for your recipe. Often, you'll want to reduce the heat to medium or medium-low after adding food to maintain a gentler simmer rather than a violent boil.
Safety tip: Always handle the pot with dry hands and use the handle. Steam can burn you just as badly as the water itself, so be careful when removing the lid - tilt it away from you to let steam escape safely.
That's all there is to it! With practice, you'll develop an intuitive sense for how long your stove takes to boil different amounts of water, making your kitchen time more efficient.

Here's how to boil water step-by-step:
Choosing your pot: Select a pot that's appropriately sized for the amount of water you need. The pot should be large enough that the water fills it about halfway to two-thirds full, leaving room to prevent boiling over. A pot with a lid will speed up the process.
Adding water: Fill your pot with the desired amount of cold water. You can use tap water or filtered water depending on your preference and intended use. Cold water actually comes to a boil faster than hot water from the tap due to the way heating dynamics work.
Placing on the stove: Set the pot on a burner that matches its size - a small pot on a large burner wastes energy, while a large pot on a small burner will heat unevenly and slowly. Make sure the pot sits flat and stable on the burner.
Turning on the heat: Turn the burner to high heat. If your pot has a lid, place it on top to trap heat and speed up the boiling process. This can reduce boiling time by several minutes.
Watching for signs: As the water heats, you'll first see small bubbles forming on the bottom of the pot - this is dissolved air being released. Next, you'll see streams of tiny bubbles rising from the bottom. The water will start to steam and move gently.
Reaching the boiling point: Water reaches its boiling point at 212°F (100°C) at sea level. You'll know it's fully boiling when large, vigorous bubbles continuously break the surface and the water is churning actively. The entire surface will be in motion with a rolling boil. At higher altitudes, water boils at lower temperatures due to decreased atmospheric pressure.
Once your water reaches this rolling boil, it's ready for whatever purpose you intended - cooking pasta, making tea, or any other use.
